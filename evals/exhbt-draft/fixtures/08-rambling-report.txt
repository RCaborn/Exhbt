GY2950 Community Placement (Volunteering and Employability), BSc Geography, University of Wessex Marches
Draft 4 (final?) - please excuse the length, I know the guidance said around 2,500 words but there was a lot to say

Introduction and some background (sorry this bit is long)

So I want to start by saying that when I signed up for the community placement module I honestly thought I would be stacking shelves for a few hours a week and writing a reflective diary about it, which is what my friend did last year at a charity shop in the town centre, and she got a decent mark for it. I did not expect to end up redesigning how a food larder keeps its records, and I definitely did not expect to spend so much of my second term thinking about spreadsheets. But that is what happened, and this report is about that, more or less, along with some other things that I think are relevant even if they are not strictly about the data.

Eastbrook Community Larder (the Larder from now on) is a small food larder run out of the back hall of St Aldric's community centre on the Eastbrook estate. I should explain that a larder is slightly different from a food bank, in that people pay a small membership fee each week (it was £3.50 when I started and went up to £4 in April, which caused a bit of discussion at the volunteer meeting, more on that later maybe) and then choose a set number of items, rather than being referred and given a parcel. The idea is that it is more dignified and people get to choose what they actually want to eat, which I agree with. The Larder is open on Tuesday and Thursday afternoons and Saturday mornings, and it is run almost entirely by volunteers, with one part-time coordinator, who I will call J because she asked me not to use her full name in anything that would be marked by the university.

The Larder has been going since 2019, apparently, although it closed for a while and then reopened, and I think the current set-up dates from about 2021. There are roughly 40 people on the volunteer list but in practice maybe 20 to 25 who come regularly. When I started in late January there were a lot of things that worked well, mainly because the volunteers are very committed and know the regular members by name, and some things that clearly did not work well, which is where my project comes in.

How the project came about

In my first two or three weeks I was mostly on the door, which meant signing people in on a paper sheet on a clipboard. Each member had to find their name on a printed list (there were about nine pages of it, in an order that was supposed to be alphabetical but wasn't really, because new people were added at the end in pen) and then we ticked them off and wrote the time, and then they waited to be called through to the hall to choose their items. On busy days, especially Saturday mornings, the queue went right down the corridor and out past the toilets, and people were waiting a long time, and some people with kids or with health problems found that really hard. One Saturday in early February it was raining and the queue was outside, and a lady left without getting her shopping, and J was quite upset about it. I remember that day quite clearly because it was also the day the boiler in the community centre broke, so the hall was freezing, and someone brought in a fan heater which then tripped the fuse. Anyway.

I said to J that I thought we could probably make the sign-in faster with a tablet and a simple form, and she said that a previous volunteer had tried something like that before (I think in 2022) using a laptop, but it had not worked because the laptop was slow and nobody knew how to fix it when it went wrong and the Wi-Fi in the back hall is terrible. So she was a bit doubtful, but she said I could try if I made sure it would still work when the Wi-Fi went down, which turned out to be a very important point.

At the same time J mentioned that the Larder had a problem with food going out of date. Donations come in from a couple of supermarkets, from the regional surplus food charity and from individual people dropping things off, and a lot of it is short-dated. Things were being put on the shelves without anyone keeping track of the dates, and then food was being found past its date and thrown away. J did not know how much, only that it was "too much". There was also a problem with the volunteer rota, which was a paper sheet on the noticeboard plus a WhatsApp group, and shifts were often left unfilled until the last minute, so J was always messaging people the night before.

So the project grew into three parts: the sign-in, the stock dates, and the rota. My placement supervisor at the university (Dr Okafor) said that was probably too much for one placement, and she was probably right, but in the end I did manage all three, though the rota part was the last and was the least finished, as I will explain.

A note on my working title

I called the project "sorting out the data" in my first progress report and it kind of stuck, although Dr Okafor said that it undersold it. I considered "Digitising Eastbrook" but it sounded like a council initiative. I am still not sure about the title so I have left it as it was.

Part 1: The sign-in

What I actually did was this. I set up a form on a tablet (the Larder already had an old tablet that someone had donated and that was sitting in a drawer in the office, a ten inch one, I won't name the brand) and linked it to a spreadsheet. Members type the first few letters of their surname, or scan a small card with a QR code on it, and the form fills in their membership number, and then it logs the time. The important part, because of the Wi-Fi problem, was that the form works offline and then syncs when the connection comes back. I tested this at home by turning my router off a lot, which my housemates were not pleased about, especially Dan who was in the middle of an online game the first time.

Making the member cards took longer than the form. We had about 380 members on the paper list but when I went through it, a lot of them had not been in for over a year, and some were duplicates (the same person written down twice with different spellings, or a married name and a maiden name). After cleaning, there were 291 active members. I printed cards for all of them on card stock from the stationery cupboard, and laminated them using the community centre's laminator, which took two whole Tuesday afternoons and I burned my thumb on it twice.

The first week of using the tablet was not great to be honest. On the first Saturday, the tablet battery died at about half past ten because nobody had charged it, and we had to go back to paper for the rest of the morning. After that we bought a long charging cable (£7, from the Larder's petty cash) so it could stay plugged in at the desk. Some of the older volunteers were nervous about using the tablet, and one volunteer, who has been there since the very beginning, refused to use it for the first fortnight and kept a paper list "just in case", which in fairness was useful on the day the battery died.

The thing that made the biggest difference was actually not the tablet itself but splitting the queue. Because the tablet logged arrival times, we could call people through in arrival order without them having to stand in a line, so people could sit down in the café area of the community centre and wait there. And we could see in the spreadsheet how long people were waiting. Before the change, J had timed waiting on two Saturdays with a stopwatch and a clipboard (literally) and the average wait from arriving to being called through was 34 minutes. After the new system had been running for six weeks, the average wait, calculated from the time stamps, was 12 minutes. The longest wait we recorded after the change was 31 minutes, on the Saturday before the Easter weekend, which was a very unusual day.

I should be honest that the before and after are not measured in exactly the same way, because the before figure was timed by hand on just two days and the after figure comes from the time stamps over six weeks, so the comparison is not perfect. Dr Okafor pointed this out in my mid-placement review and I think it is a fair point. But everyone at the Larder agrees the waits are much shorter, and the queue no longer goes out into the corridor.

Between February and the end of June the system recorded 2,316 visits. That number is interesting in itself because before this nobody really knew how many visits there were. J had been reporting a rough figure of "about 100 a week" to the funders, which turned out to be fairly close actually, because 2,316 over about 21 weeks is a bit over 100 a week, but now there is a real number and it can be broken down by day. Saturdays are the busiest by quite a way.

A side issue with the sign-in was data protection. I had to think about what personal data we were collecting and where it was stored. I spoke to the person at the community centre who deals with that (she is also the treasurer of the bowls club, which meets in the same hall on Wednesdays, which is how I ended up knowing a lot more about bowls than I ever expected to). We agreed that we would only store the membership number, first name, postcode area and visit times, not full addresses, and that the spreadsheet would only be shared with J and the two trustees. I wrote a one-page privacy note which is now on the wall by the door. This took up a surprising amount of time, maybe three weeks of back and forth by email (I counted 47 emails in that thread alone).

Part 2: Stock and dates

This was the part I was most interested in because it connected to my dissertation idea about food waste, though I have since changed my dissertation topic to something about flood risk, so that connection did not really go anywhere in the end. Still, it was the most interesting part of the placement for me.

The problem was that nobody knew what was on the shelves or when it went out of date. My first idea was a full stock system where every item was scanned in and out, but J said, very sensibly, that there was no way the volunteers would have time to scan everything, especially on a Saturday when people are bringing in bags and bags of donations. So instead I set up something simpler. When donations are sorted (on Monday evenings and Wednesday mornings), the sorting volunteers put each batch into a crate with a coloured label for the week of its use-by or best-before date, and they enter just the crate, the colour and a rough count into a second tab of the spreadsheet. Then there is a "use this first" list that shows which colours are closest to their dates, and that gets printed on a sheet of paper and stuck on the end of the shelves, so the people stocking the shelves know what to put out at the front.

The colour system was actually J's idea, from a place she volunteered at years ago. I just made the spreadsheet part. There were some arguments about which colours to use, because the first set of labels we bought had two colours that looked almost the same (a blue and a purple) under the hall lights, and one volunteer is colour blind and could not tell the red from the green, so we ended up adding a big letter to each label as well. We used six colours in the end. I also built a little chart on the dashboard tab that shows how many crates are in each colour, which J says she looks at every Monday now.

To measure food waste, the sorting volunteers weighed what was thrown away each week on the old kitchen scales and wrote it down, and we compared it with the weight of donations coming in, which we already roughly knew because the regional charity weighs what it delivers and a lot of the supermarket donations come with weights on the paperwork. For the four weeks before the colour crates, the share of donated food thrown away because it was out of date was 18%. For the last four weeks of my placement, with the colour crates and the "use this first" sheet, it was 7%. Again, the measurement is rough, because the scales are not very accurate and individual donations are estimated, but the drop is big enough that I think it is real. J was really pleased about this one because it is something she can tell the supermarkets and the funders.

A thing I did not expect was that the "use this first" sheet also changed what the members chose, because the volunteers started putting a small "best eaten this week" sign on the short-dated items and people often chose those. A couple of members told me they liked it because they could plan their week's meals. This was not planned at all, it just happened.

Also on stock, there was an ongoing debate about tinned tomatoes. The Larder always has far too many tinned tomatoes, because people donate them more than anything else. At one point we had 214 tins of tomatoes in the store room. This has nothing to do with my project really, but I mention it because it shows that the data can tell you things that are useful for the appeals that go out to donors, and J now puts a "we don't need any more tomatoes, thank you!" note in the newsletter, which has helped. We also learned we were always short of cooking oil, toilet roll and tins of meat, which did end up in the appeal.

Part 3: The rota

The rota was the last thing I worked on and it is the least finished. Before the change, shifts were on a paper sheet on the noticeboard, and people wrote their names in, and also said in the WhatsApp group whether they were coming, and the two did not always match. J spent a lot of time chasing people. Using the sign-up sheets from the previous three months, I worked out that only 64% of shifts were filled a week ahead, and the rest were filled at the last minute or not at all.

I set up a shared online rota (again a tab in the spreadsheet, with a simple form for volunteers to sign up and drop out), with a reminder message that goes out automatically to the WhatsApp group on a Sunday evening showing which shifts for the coming week were still empty. Some volunteers do not use smartphones, so J still writes their shifts on the paper sheet and then types them in. Over the last eight weeks of the placement, 89% of shifts were filled a week ahead. I think the reminder message is doing most of the work, rather than the rota itself, but either way J says she spends much less time chasing people, maybe an hour a week less, though she did not measure that, so I would not put too much weight on it.

What I would have liked to do, if I had more time, is link the rota to the visit numbers, so that the Larder could put more volunteers on at the busiest times. The data is there now, it just needs someone to do it.

Training

None of this would work if I was the only person who knew how to use it, which was the problem with the 2022 laptop. So in May I ran three short training sessions (on two Tuesday afternoons and one Saturday after closing) and trained 14 volunteers to use the sign-in tablet, the crate spreadsheet and the rota. I wrote a six-page guide with screenshots, which is in a ring binder in the office, and a shorter laminated sheet (yes, the laminator again) next to the tablet with what to do if it goes wrong, the most common thing being "plug it in". Two of the volunteers I trained are students from the sixth form college and they are now kind of the tech people, which I think is good for the long term because they will be around for at least another year.

Some reflections (for the employability part of the module)

I learned a lot about working with people who have very different levels of confidence with technology, and about not assuming that the more technical solution is better. My first ideas were all too complicated. The colour crates are a good example: the clever part was J's, not mine, and the spreadsheet just supports it. I also learned that I need to be better at keeping my supervisor updated, because there were about three weeks in March when I did not send a progress update, partly because I had two coursework deadlines and partly because the data protection emails were taking all my energy, and Dr Okafor (fairly) chased me.

I also got better at explaining numbers to people who are not interested in numbers. At the trustees' meeting in June I presented the main results, and I learned that the trustees cared most about the waiting time and the food waste, and not at all about my chart of visits by hour, which I had spent ages on. In the meeting I had planned twelve slides, and I got through five before the questions started, and in the end the questions were more useful than the slides would have been.

Things that went wrong (other than the battery and the colours) included: the spreadsheet getting accidentally sorted by one column only, which scrambled the rows (we restored it from the version history, which I had not known existed until then, and now I make a weekly copy); one of the supermarkets changing its delivery day without telling anyone, which messed up the crate counts for a fortnight; and the community centre's Wi-Fi password being changed by the bowls club, which meant the tablet stopped syncing for nearly a week before anyone noticed, because it was still working offline. That last one actually showed the offline mode worked, so I suppose it was a kind of test.

The cost of everything was very low, which I think matters for a small charity. The tablet was donated, the spreadsheet and form are free, the coloured labels cost £18 for a big pack, the charging cable was £7, and the card stock and laminating pouches came from the community centre's cupboard. So under £30 in total, apart from my time and the volunteers' time.

Other things I did at the Larder that are not part of the project

I also helped with the Easter food appeal, which collected a lot of chocolate eggs (I think 160 or so, I did not count them carefully), helped clear out the store room in April, and went with J to a meeting of local food larders in the county town, where three other larders asked if they could copy the colour crate system, which J said yes to. I also did the door on most Saturdays throughout, so I got to know a lot of the members, and I think that helped when I was asking them to try the cards.

I also want to mention the volunteers' Christmas-in-June social, which is not relevant at all but was the best evening of the placement, and where I was given a card signed by everyone, which I will keep.

Limitations

Most of the before-and-after numbers in this report are rough. The waiting time before was timed by hand on two days. The food waste figures depend on kitchen scales and estimates of donation weights. The rota comparison uses different lengths of time before and after. And the Larder was also changing in other ways during the same months (the membership fee went up in April, which might have changed who came, and spring is normally a bit quieter than winter, J says). So I cannot say for certain that my changes caused all of the improvements. But the direction is the same for all three, and the people who run the Larder say the difference is obvious.

Conclusion

To sum up (finally!): over five months at Eastbrook Community Larder I replaced the paper sign-in with an offline-capable tablet form and member cards, set up a colour-coded crate system with a "use this first" list to track food dates, and moved the volunteer rota online with an automatic weekly reminder. Average waiting time fell from 34 minutes to 12 minutes. The share of donated food thrown away fell from 18% to 7%. Shifts filled a week ahead rose from 64% to 89%. The Larder now has a real count of its visits (2,316 between February and June) to give to its funders. I trained 14 volunteers so the system does not depend on me, and the whole thing cost under £30.

If I did it again I would start with the rota, because it affects everything else, and I would measure the "before" numbers more carefully before changing anything, because now I know how much people want to see the numbers. I would also charge the tablet.
