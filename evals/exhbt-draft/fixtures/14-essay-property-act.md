# Did the Married Women's Property Act of 1882 change who held property in England?

BA History, Year 2 essay for "Law and Society in Victorian Britain". University of Castlemere, February 2026.

Historians have long treated the 1882 Act as the moment married women became owners in their own right. This essay argues that the Act changed the law far more than it changed who held property. A right that few women had the means to use left most households as they were.

First, the Act's wording was broad. It let a wife own, buy and sell property as if she were unmarried, and it ended the rule that her earnings were her husband's (Holcombe 1983).

Second, the wealth it could protect was thin. Combs's study of probate records finds that women married after 1870 held more of their wealth in money and shares, but the women in it were shopkeepers' wives, not labourers' (Combs 2005).

Third, the courts read the Act narrowly. In case after case a wife's contract bound only her separate property, so lenders went on dealing with husbands (Shanley 1989).

The Act mattered most as a statement. Campaigners used it within a decade to argue that a woman who could own a house could also vote for the council that taxed it.

If I took this further I would read the county court records for one town, to see how often wives sued in their own names.
