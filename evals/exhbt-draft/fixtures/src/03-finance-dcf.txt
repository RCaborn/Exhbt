BSc Accounting and Finance, Year 2, University of Wessex Marches
Worth 40% of the module mark. Word limit 2,000.

Valuation of Harrowgate Outdoor plc (a fictional listed retailer created for this coursework)

1. Introduction

Harrowgate Outdoor plc sells walking, camping and cycling equipment through 86 UK stores and a website that now makes up 31% of sales. The brief was to value the company's shares using a discounted cash flow (DCF) model built from its published accounts, to compare the result with the market price, and to make a recommendation.

2. Starting point

Revenue in the year to 31 January 2025 was £412.6m and operating profit (EBIT) was £45.4m, an EBIT margin of 11.0%. Capital expenditure was £18.2m against depreciation of £14.9m. Net debt at the year end was £96.0m, excluding leases. There are 120 million shares in issue, and the share price on the valuation date (14 March 2025) was £3.10.

3. Forecast assumptions

I forecast free cash flow for five years (FY2026 to FY2030):
- Revenue growth of 6% a year for the first three years, as the online share keeps rising and four new stores open, then 4% in years four and five.
- EBIT margin rising from 11.0% to 11.5% by FY2028 because online sales carry lower store costs, then held at 11.5%.
- Corporation tax at 25%.
- Capital expenditure at 4.5% of revenue, and depreciation at 3.6% of revenue.
- Working capital investment equal to 10% of the increase in revenue.

4. Discount rate

I used a weighted average cost of capital (WACC) of 9.2%. The cost of equity of 10.6% comes from the CAPM with a risk-free rate of 4.4% (the 10-year gilt yield on the valuation date), an equity risk premium of 5.0% and a beta of 1.24, the average of four listed UK leisure retailers. The pre-tax cost of debt is 6.8%, giving 5.1% after tax, and the target debt weighting is 25%.

5. Terminal value

Terminal value uses the Gordon growth model with long-run growth of 2.0%, in line with the Bank of England inflation target. Terminal value makes up 68% of the enterprise value, which is normal for a mature retailer but means the result is sensitive to the WACC and the growth assumption.

6. Result

Enterprise value: £538.0m
Less net debt: £96.0m
Equity value: £442.0m
Shares in issue: 120 million
Value per share: £3.68

The DCF value of £3.68 per share is 18.7% above the market price of £3.10.

7. Sensitivity

Changing the WACC by one percentage point either way gives a value per share of £4.31 (WACC 8.2%) and £3.21 (WACC 10.2%). With terminal growth of 1.5% instead of 2.0%, the value falls to £3.47. In every case I tested the value stays above the market price, but the margin of safety at a 10.2% WACC is small.

7.1 Cross-check

As a cross-check, the implied EV/EBIT multiple is 11.9x, against a peer median of 10.4x, so the DCF is slightly generous relative to the market's pricing of similar firms.

8. Recommendation

On these assumptions the shares look undervalued, and I would rate them a cautious buy. The main risks are that online growth slows, that the four new stores take longer to break even, and that a wet summer hits camping sales, which made up 22% of revenue last year.

9. Reflection

Building the model in Excel taught me how much the answer depends on two or three inputs. My first draft used a 3% terminal growth rate, which pushed the value above £4.50; my tutor's feedback made me justify every assumption against a source. The final mark for the coursework was 72%.
